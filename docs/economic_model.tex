\section{Purpose and timing}

The simulation is a deliberately small New Keynesian economy.  Its organizing
logic comes from the two-equation (IS and Phillips curve) model in Gal\'i
\cite{gali2015}.  At the beginning of
quarter $t$, the player observes data through $t-1$ and chooses the nominal
policy rate $i_t$.  The game then computes quarter-$t$ output, inflation, and
unemployment.  These outcomes become visible at the beginning of the next
turn.

All rates and gaps are expressed in percentage points.  The code evaluates
each displayed equation separately and in the order used below.  This makes
the timing assumptions explicit and makes alternative calibrations easy to
implement.

\section{The benchmark New Keynesian model}

The benchmark has two core equilibrium conditions.  The New Keynesian
Phillips curve (NKPC) is
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t=\beta E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}+\kappa\gap_t,
  \qquad \beta>0,\quad\kappa>0,
  \label{eq:benchmark-nkpc}
\end{equation}
and the dynamic IS equation is
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t=E_t\{\gap_{t+1}\}
  -\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(i_t-E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}-\rstar_t\right),
  \qquad \sigma>0.
  \label{eq:benchmark-is}
\end{equation}
Here $\pi_t$ is inflation, $\gap_t$ is the log deviation of output from its
flexible-price (natural) level, $\rstar_t$ is the natural real interest rate,
and $\sigma$ governs how strongly demand responds to the real-rate gap.

These equations are not arbitrary behavioral curves.  Gal\'i derives the IS
equation from the representative household's Euler equation and the NKPC from
firms' optimal price-setting policies.  A crucial step in the former
derivation---the determination of consumption---uses a log-linear
approximation around a steady state with constant inflation \cite[p.~21]{gali2015}.
It is therefore most trustworthy locally.  Large simulated departures from
the steady state need additional safeguards.

The benchmark also abstracts from investment and capital accumulation.  Its simple
Euler equation transmits the current real rate directly to current
consumption demand.  It therefore omits project planning, time to build, and
the costs of abandoning partially completed investment.  Those mechanisms
are important reasons why actual monetary transmission is distributed over
time.

\section{Modifications to the benchmark}

The two game papers play a narrower role than Gal\'i.  Aruoba and Drechsel
present Econland as a teaching environment in which a student conducts
monetary policy in a simulated economy \cite{aruoba-drechsel2024}.  Lengwiler
likewise presents a simulation in which students learn monetary policy by
making repeated interest-rate decisions \cite{lengwiler2004}.  They are design
precedents for a playable dynamic economy; neither paper turns the equations
below into structural estimates.  Accordingly, the lag and expectation rules
borrow their transparent game logic, while their numerical coefficients
remain calibration choices.

\subsection{Expectations and the current real rate}

The benchmark contains two expectations formed at $t$: next-period inflation
and the next-period output gap.  The game must replace both with observable
rules because it does not solve a full rational-expectations path after every
click.  Expected next-quarter inflation is a credibility-weighted combination
of the target and the latest observed inflation:
\begin{equation}
  E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}=a_t\pi_t^*+(1-a_t)\pi_{t-1},
  \qquad
  a_t=\min\{1,\max\{0,k_R\mathcal R_t\}\}.
  \label{eq:inflation-expectations}
\end{equation}
$\mathcal R_t$ is central-bank reputation and $k_R$ converts it into the
weight on the announced target.  The real rate associated with the player's
new choice is then
\begin{equation}
  r_t=i_t-E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}.
  \label{eq:real-rate}
\end{equation}
This is an ex-ante real rate: it uses the inflation expectation held when the
nominal rate is chosen, not inflation subsequently realized.

\subsection{Delayed monetary transmission}

The rate relevant to current spending is a stock of effective real-rate
pressure,
\begin{equation}
  R_t=\rho R_{t-1}+(1-\rho)r_{t-1},
  \qquad 0\leq\rho\leq1.
  \label{eq:effective-rate}
\end{equation}
Thus $i_t$ determines $r_t$ immediately but cannot affect output until
quarter $t+1$.  Thereafter its effect fades geometrically.  Aruoba and
Drechsel use this recursively weighted interest-rate measure in their
macroeconomics game to spread monetary transmission across periods
\cite{aruoba-drechsel2024}.  Their construction is a reduced-form device for a
game rather than a structural investment model.  We adopt it because it gives
the simulation two features absent from Gal\'i's consumption-only benchmark:
policy acts with a lag, and a past rate continues to matter after a new rate
has been chosen.

\subsection{Expected normalization of the output gap}

Rather than solve the model forward, agents are assumed to expect an observed
gap to shrink:
\begin{equation}
  E_t\{\gap_{t+1}\}=\phi\gap_{t-1},
  \qquad 0\leq\phi<1.
  \label{eq:gap-expectations}
\end{equation}
The information lag is intentional: $\gap_{t-1}$ is the latest output datum
available when the player acts.  The restriction $\phi<1$ encodes eventual
normalization without assuming that the gap is already zero.  This expectation
rule follows Lengwiler's monetary-policy simulation \cite{lengwiler2004}.  It
is deliberately adaptive, not a claim that agents possess model-consistent
rational expectations.

\subsection{Modified dynamic IS equation}

Combining the preceding assumptions gives the simulation's IS equation:
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t^{\mathrm{IS}}=
  \phi\gap_{t-1}
  -\frac{1}{\sigma}(R_t-\rstar_t)
  +\varepsilon_{d,t},
  \label{eq:game-is}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_{d,t}$ is a demand shock.  A real rate above the natural
rate lowers the output gap; an expansionary demand shock raises it.  Notice
that current inflation does not have to be solved simultaneously with output:
expected inflation entered earlier when $r_t$ was formed, and that new rate is
lagged.

\subsection{Output capacity and Okun's law}

The game's existing Okun equation translates the output gap into
unemployment:
\begin{equation}
  u_t=u_t^n-b_u\gap_t,
  \qquad b_u>0.
  \label{eq:okun}
\end{equation}
A one-percent unemployment floor implies the largest admissible output gap,
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t^{\max}=\frac{u_t^n-u^{\min}}{b_u},
  \qquad u^{\min}=1,
  \label{eq:maximum-gap}
\end{equation}
and the implemented gap is
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t=\min\{\gap_t^{\mathrm{IS}},\gap_t^{\max}\}.
  \label{eq:gap-guardrail}
\end{equation}
This is best described as a numerical and physical-capacity guardrail, not as
a result derived from the log-linear model.  It prevents a local
approximation from implying negative unemployment when the economy is very
far above potential.  Equation \eqref{eq:okun} is evaluated only after the IS
result has been capped.

\subsection{Asymmetric Phillips curve}

The simulation first changes the NKPC slope when activity is weak:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_t=
  \begin{cases}
    \kappa, & \gap_t\geq0,\\
    \lambda_x\kappa, & \gap_t<0,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad \lambda_x=\tfrac12,
  \label{eq:asymmetric-slope}
\end{equation}
then calculates raw inflation,
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}
  =\beta E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}+\kappa_t\gap_t+\varepsilon_{\pi,t}.
  \label{eq:game-nkpc}
\end{equation}
Long-lived wage and price agreements and staggered price review make nominal
adjustment gradual; Gal\'i's Calvo-price foundation supplies the standard
microeconomic rationale \cite{gali2015}.  Downward nominal wage rigidity gives
additional reason to expect adjustment to be asymmetric
\cite{daly-hobijn2014}.  The exact one-half factors are transparent game
calibrations, however, rather than coefficients implied by those papers.

If the raw result is deflation, the game halves its magnitude a second time:
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t^{\mathrm{slow}}=
  \begin{cases}
    \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}, & \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}\geq0,\\
    \lambda_\pi\pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}, & \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}<0,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad \lambda_\pi=\tfrac12.
  \label{eq:deflation-asymmetry}
\end{equation}
The two adjustments are cumulative: a negative gap receives half the normal
Phillips slope, and a negative resulting inflation rate is then halved.
Finally,
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t=\max\{\pi_t^{\mathrm{slow}},-99\}.
  \label{eq:inflation-floor}
\end{equation}
The $-99$ percent floor is a numerical guardrail consistent with the fact that
a price index cannot fall by 100 percent without reaching zero.  It is not a
claim that $-99$ percent quarterly inflation is economically plausible.

\subsection{Reported GDP growth}

The output gap is a log-level gap.  To retain the annualized GDP-growth series
shown in the interface, the game uses the accounting conversion
\begin{equation}
  g_t=g_t^p+4(\gap_t-\gap_{t-1}),
  \label{eq:growth}
\end{equation}
where four reflects quarterly turns.  This reporting equation does not feed
back into the IS equation.

\section{Order of calculation in one turn}

For clarity, a quarter is evaluated as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item form $E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}$ from information through $t-1$;
  \item combine the player's $i_t$ with that expectation to record $r_t$;
  \item update $R_t$ using $r_{t-1}$, not the newly chosen $r_t$;
  \item form the expected future gap in equation \eqref{eq:gap-expectations};
  \item evaluate the IS equation and apply the output-capacity guardrail;
  \item evaluate the asymmetric NKPC, slow any deflation, and apply its floor;
  \item use Okun's law and the growth identity to produce reported data.
\end{enumerate}
Shocks to the natural unemployment rate and natural real rate are applied
before these equations, so the current quarter uses their updated values.

\section{Default calibration}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Parameter & Default & Interpretation \\
\midrule
$\beta$ & 1.0 & weight on expected inflation \\
$\kappa$ & 0.1 & Phillips-curve slope \\
$\lambda_x$ & 0.5 & slope ratio for a negative gap \\
$\lambda_\pi$ & 0.5 & adjustment ratio for deflation \\
$\phi$ & 0.8 & expected-gap persistence \\
$\rho$ & 0.5 & effective-real-rate persistence \\
$\sigma$ & 1.0 & inverse intertemporal elasticity \\
$b_u$ & 0.7 & Okun coefficient \\
$u^{\min}$ & 1.0 & unemployment floor (percent) \\
$\pi^{\min}$ & $-99.0$ & inflation floor (percent) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
These values are starting points for gameplay, not estimates.  A later
calibration chapter can replace them without changing the equation code.
