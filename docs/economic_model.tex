\section{Purpose and timing}

The simulation is a quarterly monetary-policy game.  It borrows the organizing
ideas of the canonical New Keynesian model in Gal\'i \cite{gali2015}, but it is
not intended to be a complete structural model.  Its objective is to make the
transmission mechanism transparent, produce economically intelligible paths,
and give the player a repeated policy problem with delayed effects.  This last
objective follows the pedagogical spirit of Lengwiler's earlier monetary-policy
simulation game \cite{lengwiler2004}.

Timing is important.  At the start of quarter $t$, the player observes data
through $t-1$ and chooses the nominal policy rate $i_t$.  The simulation then
computes the outcomes dated $t$.  Those outcomes are displayed at the start of
the next turn.  The rate $i_t$ is used to construct an ex-ante real rate, but it
does not affect the output gap until $t+1$.  Thus a choice is never allowed to
change data that were conceptually determined before that choice could be
transmitted through the economy.

All inflation, interest, and growth rates are annualized percentage rates.
The output gap and unemployment rate are in percentage points.  One turn is
one quarter, so the annualized growth identity uses four quarters per year.

\section{The canonical benchmark}

Let $\gap_t$ denote the log deviation of output from its flexible-price, or
natural, level.  The two familiar equations of the basic New Keynesian model
are the New Keynesian Phillips curve
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t=\beta E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}+\kappa\gap_t,
  \qquad \kappa>0,
  \label{eq:canonical-nkpc}
\end{equation}
and the dynamic IS equation
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t=E_t\{\gap_{t+1}\}
  -\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(i_t-E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}-\rstar_t\right),
  \qquad \sigma>0.
  \label{eq:canonical-is}
\end{equation}
The natural real rate $\rstar_t$ is the real return consistent with the
flexible-price allocation.  Hence the term in parentheses is a real-rate gap:
a real policy rate above the natural rate contracts current demand.

Iterating equation \eqref{eq:canonical-is} forward and imposing the usual
terminal condition gives a representation in terms of the expected path of
future real-rate gaps.  It is therefore not quite correct to say that the
expected future output gap is simply ``removed.''  Rather, its terminal value
vanishes and the intervening expected real-rate gaps remain.  That forward
solution is useful theoretically, but it asks a game to construct an entire
expected future policy path.  The simulation instead keeps a simple, explicit
expectation for the next output gap.

\subsection{Why the game departs from the benchmark}

The benchmark is deliberately spare.  In its textbook form, private demand is
represented by household consumption.  It consequently omits mechanisms such
as investment planning, financing, construction time, and costly cancellation
of projects already under way.  Those mechanisms are among the reasons that
past financial conditions may affect current activity.  The absence of such a
channel in equation \eqref{eq:canonical-is} is inconvenient in a game intended
to teach delayed monetary transmission.

The canonical equations are also log-linear approximations around a steady
state.  They are most credible for modest fluctuations near that point.  A
player, unlike the representative agent in a local approximation, can select
extreme rates and can drive the simulated economy far from equilibrium.  The
model therefore adds two explicit feasibility guardrails.  These should be
read as safeguards for a teaching simulation, not as propositions derived
from household or firm optimization.

\section{Expectations used by the simulation}

Expected inflation is a weighted average of the announced target and the most
recent observed inflation rate:
\begin{align}
  E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}&=\alpha_t\pistar_t+(1-\alpha_t)\pi_{t-1},
  \label{eq:inflation-expectations}\\
  \alpha_t&=\min\{1,\max\{0,k_a A_t\}\}.
  \label{eq:expectation-weight}
\end{align}
Here $A_t$ is central-bank reputation and $k_a$ translates it into the weight
placed on the target.  This is a bounded, adaptive expectation rather than a
model-consistent rational expectation.  The simplification lets credibility
matter without requiring the player or the computer to announce and solve a
complete future policy path.

At the moment the quarter is solved, $\gap_t$ is not yet known.  Agents are
assumed to expect the last observed gap to close gradually:
\begin{equation}
  E_t\{\gap_{t+1}\}=\phi\gap_{t-1},
  \qquad 0\leq\phi\leq1.
  \label{eq:output-gap-expectations}
\end{equation}
This parsimonious rule also resembles the inertial forecasting device needed
in a simulation game such as Lengwiler's.  It is not claimed to be the unique
or fully rational forecast.  A higher $\phi$ means that booms and recessions
are expected to persist; a lower $\phi$ means quicker expected mean reversion.

\section{Delayed monetary transmission}

First define the ex-ante real rate selected in quarter $t$ and its gap from the
natural rate:
\begin{align}
  r_t&=i_t-E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\},
  \label{eq:real-rate}\\
  q_t&=r_t-\rstar_t.
  \label{eq:real-rate-gap}
\end{align}
The simulation records both $r_t$ and $\rstar_t$ when quarter $t$ is completed.
It does not reconstruct an old real rate using subsequently realized
inflation.

Current demand responds to a geometrically smoothed stock of past real-rate
gaps:
\begin{equation}
  R_t=\rho R_{t-1}+(1-\rho)q_{t-1},
  \qquad 0\leq\rho\leq1.
  \label{eq:effective-real-rate-gap}
\end{equation}
This notation makes one correction to the informal expression
$R_t=\rho R_{t-1}+(1-\rho)r_{t-1}$: the IS equation must respond to monetary
restraint relative to the natural rate, so the input is $q_{t-1}$ rather than
the raw real rate.  Otherwise a constant positive natural rate would cause a
permanent recession even under neutral policy.

Aruoba and Drechsel \cite{aruoba-drechsel2024} document that the responses of
disaggregated price indices to monetary policy are heterogeneous and can arrive
with long and variable lags.  Their evidence supports taking delayed
transmission seriously, but it does not uniquely imply the geometric recursion
in equation \eqref{eq:effective-real-rate-gap}.  That recursion is a transparent
and editable game approximation.  It is also in the spirit of Lengwiler
\cite{lengwiler2004}, whose simulation makes policy affect the economy with a
lag.  With $0<\rho<1$, every past rate gap remains relevant with a geometrically
declining weight.

The modified dynamic IS equation is
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t^{\mathrm{IS}}
  =E_t\{\gap_{t+1}\}
  -\frac{\chi}{\sigma}R_t+\varepsilon_{d,t},
  \qquad \chi>0,
  \label{eq:modified-is}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_{d,t}$ is a demand shock and $\chi$ is an editable scale on
the interest-rate channel.  Substitution of equation
\eqref{eq:output-gap-expectations} yields the equation computed by the game:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\gap_t^{\mathrm{IS}}
  =\phi\gap_{t-1}-\frac{\chi}{\sigma}R_t+\varepsilon_{d,t}.}
  \label{eq:implemented-is}
\end{equation}
The current choice $i_t$ is absent from equation \eqref{eq:implemented-is}.
It creates $q_t$, which enters $R_{t+1}$ and therefore first affects output in
the next quarter.

\section{Capacity guardrail and unemployment}

Okun's law is applied after output is determined:
\begin{equation}
  u_t=u_t^n-\lambda_u\gap_t,
  \qquad \lambda_u>0,
  \label{eq:okun}
\end{equation}
where $u_t^n$ is the current natural unemployment rate.  The game imposes
$u^{\min}=1$ percent.  Combining that floor with equation \eqref{eq:okun}
gives the largest feasible output gap:
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t^{\max}=\frac{u_t^n-u^{\min}}{\lambda_u}.
  \label{eq:maximum-output-gap}
\end{equation}
The implemented gap is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t=\min\{\gap_t^{\mathrm{IS}},\gap_t^{\max}\}.
  \label{eq:capacity-clipping}
\end{equation}
This one-sided clipping is a feasibility guardrail for large positive
departures, where the linear IS and Okun relations would otherwise imply zero
or negative unemployment.  It is preferable to describing the device as a
precise structural nonlinearity: the one-percent number is a transparent
simulation boundary, not an estimated full-employment threshold.  Negative
output gaps remain unconstrained by this equation, while unemployment has a
separate upper display bound to protect the application from extreme inputs.

\section{Inflation and downward rigidity}

The game begins from the New Keynesian Phillips-curve logic but adds a cost-push
shock and two explicit asymmetries.  First define the gap slope
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_t=
  \begin{cases}
    \kappa, & \gap_t\geq0,\\
    \frac{1}{2}\kappa, & \gap_t<0.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:asymmetric-slope}
\end{equation}
Inflation before the deflation guardrail is
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}
  =\beta E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}+\kappa_t\gap_t+\varepsilon_{\pi,t}.
  \label{eq:raw-phillips}
\end{equation}
Thus an equally sized negative output gap exerts half as much disinflationary
pressure as a positive gap exerts inflationary pressure.  If the entire right
side---including expectations and the cost-push shock---is negative, the game
then halves the resulting deflation:
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t^{\mathrm{slow}}=
  \begin{cases}
    \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}, & \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}\geq0,\\
    \frac{1}{2}\pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}, & \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}<0.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:deflation-slowdown}
\end{equation}
Finally,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\pi_t=\max\{-99,\pi_t^{\mathrm{slow}}\}.}
  \label{eq:inflation-floor}
\end{equation}
For an inflation rate interpreted as a percentage change, a value at or below
$-100$ percent would imply a nonpositive price level.  The $-99$ percent floor
keeps the simulation on the economically meaningful side of that boundary.

Long-lived nominal contracts, infrequent price review, menu costs, and
resistance to nominal wage cuts all provide reasons for downward adjustment to
be sluggish.  Standard Calvo pricing in the canonical model establishes
nominal rigidity, but it does not by itself deliver the exact one-half
asymmetries above.  Those ratios are deliberately simple gameplay assumptions,
not estimates attributed to Gal\'i or to Aruoba and Drechsel.  Stating that
limitation is important: the empirical paper supports heterogeneous and delayed
price responses, whereas the direction and magnitude of these two asymmetries
are model-design choices.

\section{Variables reported to the player}

The model determines the output-level gap and inflation sequentially.  Actual
annualized GDP growth is recovered from
\begin{equation}
  g_t=g_t^p+4(\gap_t-\gap_{t-1}),
  \label{eq:growth-gap-identity}
\end{equation}
and unemployment is then obtained from equation \eqref{eq:okun}.  This keeps a
level gap distinct from a growth rate: an unchanged positive gap implies growth
at potential, not permanently above-potential growth.

The complete quarterly calculation follows the same order as the code:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item form inflation and output-gap expectations;
  \item update $R_t$ using the rate gap recorded in $t-1$;
  \item calculate the unconstrained gap from the modified IS equation;
  \item apply the capacity gap implied by one-percent unemployment;
  \item calculate raw inflation with the appropriate Phillips-curve slope;
  \item slow any deflation and apply the $-99$ percent floor;
  \item calculate unemployment and reported GDP growth; and
  \item record $i_t$, its ex-ante real rate, and all outcomes for the next turn.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Steady state and interpretation}

With no shocks, suppose $\gap_{t-1}=0$, expected inflation is constant, and the
lagged real policy rate equals the natural rate.  Equations
\eqref{eq:effective-real-rate-gap} and \eqref{eq:implemented-is} give
$R_t=0$ and $\gap_t=0$.  With the default $\beta=1$, equation
\eqref{eq:raw-phillips} gives $\pi_t=E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}$.  Okun's law gives
$u_t=u_t^n$, and equation \eqref{eq:growth-gap-identity} gives $g_t=g_t^p$.
Neutral policy therefore preserves a steady state at any constant expected
inflation rate; reputation gradually determines whether expectations remain
there or move toward the announced target.

This steady-state result also explains two implementation choices.  Monetary
pressure is defined as a gap from $\rstar$, not as the real rate itself, and the
default Phillips expectation coefficient is one.  A conventional calibrated
quarterly discount factor below one can be selected later, but doing so in this
reduced-form game would require an intercept or a different expectation law to
preserve positive steady inflation exactly.
