\section{Purpose and timing}

The simulation is a quarterly monetary-policy game.  Its organizing framework
is the canonical New Keynesian model in Gal\'i \cite{gali2015}, adapted to make
policy lags visible and to keep extreme player choices inside economically
meaningful bounds.  Like Lengwiler's monetary-policy simulation
\cite{lengwiler2004}, it is a teaching model rather than a complete structural
account of the economy.

At the beginning of quarter $t$, the player sees observations through $t-1$ and
chooses the nominal policy rate $i_t$.  The program then calculates the outcomes
for $t$, which become visible on the following turn.  The newly selected rate is
used to record an ex-ante real rate for quarter $t$, but it first affects the
output gap in quarter $t+1$.  Inflation, interest, and unemployment rates are
reported in percentage points; one turn represents one quarter.

\section{The basic model}

The canonical model has two central equations.  The New Keynesian Phillips
curve is
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t=\beta E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}+\kappa\gap_t,
  \qquad 0<\beta\leq1,\quad \kappa>0,
  \label{eq:canonical-nkpc}
\end{equation}
and the dynamic IS equation is
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t=E_t\{\gap_{t+1}\}
  -\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(i_t-E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}-\rstar_t\right),
  \qquad \sigma>0.
  \label{eq:canonical-is}
\end{equation}
The notation is as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\pi_t$ is inflation in quarter $t$, and $E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}$ is the
  inflation rate expected for the next quarter using information available at
  $t$.
  \item $\gap_t$ is the output gap: the log deviation of actual output from its
  flexible-price, or natural, level.  A positive value denotes an economy above
  its sustainable level; a negative value denotes slack.
  \item $E_t\{\gap_{t+1}\}$ is the expected next-quarter output gap.
  \item $i_t$ is the nominal policy interest rate selected by the central bank.
  \item $\rstar_t$ is the natural real interest rate, meaning the real return
  consistent with the flexible-price allocation.  Therefore
  $i_t-E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}-\rstar_t$ measures monetary restraint relative to
  neutral policy.
  \item $\beta$ is the household discount factor.  In the structural model it
  discounts expected future inflation and is normally positive and close to
  one at a quarterly frequency.
  \item $\kappa$ is the Phillips-curve slope.  It summarizes how strongly
  inflation responds to the output gap and, in the canonical derivation,
  depends on price stickiness and other structural features.
  \item $\sigma$ is the inverse intertemporal elasticity of substitution.  It
  governs how strongly household consumption, and hence the output gap in the
  consumption-only benchmark, reacts to the real interest-rate gap.
\end{itemize}

Equation \eqref{eq:canonical-is} says that a real policy rate above the natural
rate reduces current demand.  Equation \eqref{eq:canonical-nkpc} says that a
positive output gap raises inflation because scarce capacity increases firms'
marginal costs.  Expected future conditions matter in both equations.

The benchmark is intentionally spare.  Private expenditure is represented by
consumption, so investment planning, construction time, financing, and the
costly cancellation of projects already under way are absent.  Those omitted
mechanisms are important reasons why past financial conditions can continue to
affect activity.  The equations are also log-linear approximations around a
steady state and are most reliable for modest departures from it.  A game in
which the player can choose extreme rates therefore needs transparent
additional safeguards.

Iterating equation \eqref{eq:canonical-is} forward under the usual terminal
condition expresses the current gap as a function of expected future real-rate
gaps.  The expected future output gap is not simply discarded: its terminal
value vanishes while the expected path of real-rate gaps remains.  That forward
solution is useful theoretically, but it would require the game to specify a
complete expected future policy path.  The adaptation below instead uses a
simple expectation rule.

\subsection{Translating the output gap into unemployment}

The player observes the economy primarily through inflation and unemployment,
whereas the basic New Keynesian equations use the output gap.  The game connects
the two with the gap version of Okun's law:
\begin{equation}
  u_t-u_t^n=-\lambda_u\gap_t,
  \qquad \lambda_u>0,
  \label{eq:okun-gap}
\end{equation}
or, equivalently,
\begin{equation}
  u_t=u_t^n-\lambda_u\gap_t.
  \label{eq:okun-level}
\end{equation}
Here $u_t$ is unemployment, $u_t^n$ is the natural unemployment rate, and
$\lambda_u$ is the Okun coefficient.  A positive output gap puts unemployment
below its natural rate; a negative output gap puts it above the natural rate.
Okun's law is evaluated after the output gap is determined.

\section{Adaptations and guardrails}

\subsection{Simple expectations}

Expected inflation is a weighted average of the central bank's announced
target and the latest observed inflation rate:
\begin{equation}
  E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}=\alpha_t\pistar_t+(1-\alpha_t)\pi_{t-1},
  \label{eq:inflation-expectations}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_t=A_t k_a,
  \qquad 0\leq A_t\leq1,\quad 0\leq k_a\leq1.
  \label{eq:expectation-weight}
\end{equation}
$A_t$ is central-bank reputation and $k_a$ controls how strongly reputation
anchors expectations.  Greater reputation therefore shifts more weight from
recent inflation toward the target.  This is an adaptive expectation rule, not
a claim that agents possess model-consistent rational expectations.

When quarter $t$ is calculated, $\gap_t$ is not yet known.  Agents are assumed
to expect the last observed gap to close gradually:
\begin{equation}
  E_t\{\gap_{t+1}\}=\phi\gap_{t-1},
  \qquad 0\leq\phi\leq1.
  \label{eq:output-gap-expectations}
\end{equation}
A larger $\phi$ represents more persistent expected booms and recessions.  This
kind of inertial forecasting rule is also natural in a simulation game such as
Lengwiler's \cite{lengwiler2004}.

\subsection{Delayed monetary transmission}

The ex-ante real interest rate selected in quarter $t$ is
\begin{equation}
  r_t=i_t-E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}.
  \label{eq:real-rate}
\end{equation}
The game records this expectation when the rate is selected; it does not later
reconstruct the old real rate using realized inflation.

Current demand responds to a geometrically smoothed stock of past real-rate
gaps:
\begin{equation}
  R_t=\rho R_{t-1}
  +(1-\rho)(r_{t-1}-r^n_{t-1}),
  \qquad 0\leq\rho\leq1.
  \label{eq:effective-real-rate-gap}
\end{equation}
Writing the natural-rate difference directly keeps the economic meaning clear:
a positive value is restrictive and a negative value is accommodative.  It is
essential to subtract $r^n_{t-1}$; using the raw real rate would make a positive
natural rate contractionary even when policy is neutral.

Aruoba and Drechsel \cite{aruoba-drechsel2024} find that disaggregated prices
respond to monetary policy at heterogeneous horizons and that important effects
can emerge after long and variable lags.  Their evidence motivates delayed
transmission, but it does not uniquely derive the geometric recursion in
equation \eqref{eq:effective-real-rate-gap}.  The recursion is a deliberately
simple and editable approximation for the game.  Lengwiler's simulation also
makes monetary-policy effects arrive with a lag \cite{lengwiler2004}.

The adapted dynamic IS equation is
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t^{\mathrm{IS}}
  =E_t\{\gap_{t+1}\}-\frac{1}{\widetilde\sigma}R_t
  +\varepsilon_{d,t},
  \qquad \widetilde\sigma>0,
  \label{eq:modified-is}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_{d,t}$ is a demand shock.  We intend to use the same default
numerical value for $\widetilde\sigma$ as for $\sigma$ in Gal\'i.  The tilde
makes clear that its interpretation is no longer identical: it scales the
response to a smoothed stock of lagged real-rate gaps rather than the response
of current consumption to the contemporaneous real-rate gap.  Combining
 equations \eqref{eq:output-gap-expectations} and \eqref{eq:modified-is} gives
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t^{\mathrm{IS}}
  =\phi\gap_{t-1}-\frac{1}{\widetilde\sigma}R_t
  +\varepsilon_{d,t}.
  \label{eq:implemented-is}
\end{equation}
The current choice $i_t$ is absent: it determines $r_t$, which first enters
$R_{t+1}$ and affects the gap in the following quarter.

\subsection{An upper bound on the output gap}

The local IS approximation can otherwise imply negative unemployment after an
extreme stimulus.  The game therefore imposes a minimum unemployment rate
$u^{\min}=1$ percent.  Combining this boundary with Okun's law gives
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t^{\max}=\frac{u_t^n-u^{\min}}{\lambda_u},
  \label{eq:maximum-output-gap}
\end{equation}
and the implemented output gap is
\begin{equation}
  \gap_t=\min\{\gap_t^{\mathrm{IS}},\gap_t^{\max}\}.
  \label{eq:capacity-clipping}
\end{equation}
This is a transparent numerical feasibility guardrail, not an estimated
structural full-employment threshold.  It constrains only implausibly large
positive gaps; recessions are not clipped by this equation.

\subsection{Phillips curve and a deflation floor}

The same Phillips slope is used during inflation and disinflation. Inflation
before the final deflation guardrail is
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}
  =\beta E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}+\kappa\gap_t
  +\varepsilon_{\pi,t},
  \label{eq:raw-phillips}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_{\pi,t}$ is a cost-push shock. When this complete expression
is negative, the deflation slowdown ratio $d$ reduces its magnitude:
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t^{\mathrm{slow}}=
  \begin{cases}
    \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}, & \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}\geq0,\\
    d\pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}, & \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}<0,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad 0<d\leq1.
\end{equation}
Finally,
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t=\max\{-99,\pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}\}.
  \label{eq:inflation-floor}
\end{equation}
The $-99$ percent floor prevents a percentage-change
interpretation from implying a nonpositive price level.

\subsection{The complete modified model}

The adaptations can be collected in one compact sequence:
\begin{align}
 E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}&=A_tk_a\pistar_t+(1-A_tk_a)\pi_{t-1},
 &&\label{eq:final-inflation-expectation}\\
 E_t\{\gap_{t+1}\}&=\phi\gap_{t-1},
 &&\label{eq:final-gap-expectation}\\
 r_t&=i_t-E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\},
 &&\label{eq:final-real-rate}\\
 R_t&=\rho R_{t-1}+(1-\rho)(r_{t-1}-r^n_{t-1}),
 &&\label{eq:final-rate-stock}\\
 \gap_t^{\mathrm{IS}}&=\phi\gap_{t-1}-R_t/\widetilde\sigma
 +\varepsilon_{d,t},
 &&\label{eq:final-is}\\
 \gap_t&=\min\!\left\{\gap_t^{\mathrm{IS}},
 (u_t^n-u^{\min})/\lambda_u\right\},
 &&\label{eq:final-capacity}\\
 \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}&=\beta E_t\{\pi_{t+1}\}
 +\kappa_t\gap_t+\varepsilon_{\pi,t},
 &&\label{eq:final-phillips}\\
 \pi_t^{\mathrm{slow}}&=
 \begin{cases}
 \pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}},&\pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}\geq0,\\
 \delta_\pi\pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}},&\pi_t^{\mathrm{raw}}<0,
 \end{cases}
 &&\label{eq:final-slow-inflation}\\
 \pi_t&=\max\{-99,\pi_t^{\mathrm{slow}}\},
 &&\label{eq:final-inflation}\\
 u_t-u_t^n&=-\lambda_u\gap_t.
 &&\label{eq:final-okun}
\end{align}
In equations \eqref{eq:final-phillips}--\eqref{eq:final-inflation},
$\kappa_t=\kappa$ for a nonnegative gap and
$\kappa_t=\delta_y\kappa$ for a negative gap.  The code evaluates these
relations in the order shown, with one separate function for each substantive
step.

\section{Default parameter values}

Table \ref{tab:parameters} reports the initial calibration.  These are
transparent gameplay defaults, not estimates.  They are deliberately moderate
so that policy has a visible but not explosive effect and can be revised in a
later calibration chapter.

The fallback expected inflation rate is 2 percent.  Natural unemployment and
the natural real rate revert slowly toward anchors of 5 percent and 0.5 percent,
with quarterly adjustment speeds of 0.02.  Their lower natural-unemployment
boundary is 2 percent.  At the highest difficulty, the quarterly standard
deviations of the inflation, demand, natural-unemployment, and natural-real-rate
shocks are respectively $(0.3,0.285714,0.05,0)$; easier modes scale all four down.
The innovations are contemporaneously uncorrelated by default, while the full
correlation-matrix structure remains available for future calibrations.
The settings screen exposes these quantities so future maintainers can adjust
them without changing equation code.

With no shocks, $\gap_{t-1}=0$, constant expected inflation, and a history of
neutral real rates, the model gives $R_t=0$, $\gap_t=0$, $\pi_t=E_t\pi_{t+1}$,
and $u_t=u_t^n$.  Thus the defaults preserve a neutral steady state at any
constant expected inflation rate; credibility governs whether expectations
remain there or move toward the announced target.
